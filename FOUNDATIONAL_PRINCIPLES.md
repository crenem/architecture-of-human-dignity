# Foundational Principles Charter
## Architecture of Human Dignity — Draft 0.1

> **Mission:** Develop practical, transparent, and contestable methods for designing and evaluating artificial intelligence in service of human dignity, justice, solidarity, peace, and the common good.

## 1. Scope and standing

This Charter is a philosophical and governance statement, **not a legal license**. It explains the ethical commitments motivating the project and guides research, design, evaluation, and community deliberation. The enforceable conditions, if any, are only those stated in [LICENSE.md](LICENSE.md); this Charter does not add restrictions, amend grants, or create an independent basis for license termination.

The project draws explicitly on Catholic social teaching (CST), while welcoming participants of every faith and none. No religious profession or assent is required. Catholic sources are cited as theological sources, not as evidence that the Church has endorsed this project.

## 2. Foundational commitments

### 2.1 Inherent human dignity
Every human person possesses dignity that is not earned through productivity, ability, wealth, citizenship, health, or social status. AI must not treat people merely as optimization targets.

**Design implications:** Make foreseeable impacts on persons visible; provide accessible explanations and avenues to challenge consequential outcomes; assess risks to privacy, bodily integrity, and agency.

### 2.2 The common good
Systems should support social conditions in which persons and communities can flourish, not merely maximize the advantage of the operator or immediate user.

**Design implications:** Evaluate externalities, institutional effects, and unequal distribution of costs and benefits; avoid reducing welfare to a single metric.

### 2.3 Solidarity
Human beings are interdependent. The interests of those exposed to harm should be considered as seriously as the interests of those who commission a system.

**Design implications:** Consult affected communities, especially those ordinarily excluded from product design; document whose perspectives are missing.

### 2.4 Subsidiarity
Decisions should remain with persons and institutions capable of responsibly making them at the appropriate level. AI should support, rather than needlessly displace, human judgment.

**Design implications:** Define decision authority; establish meaningful oversight and escalation; avoid nominal human approval that merely rubber-stamps automation.

### 2.5 Preferential option for people experiencing poverty
The needs and vulnerabilities of people facing deprivation deserve particular attention when systems distribute opportunity, risk, and resources.

**Design implications:** Test for exclusion by cost, connectivity, literacy, disability, and access to representation; investigate disparate burdens rather than assuming average performance is sufficient.

### 2.6 Rights and responsibilities
Human rights, due process, privacy, participation, and reciprocal duties constrain technological power.

**Design implications:** Apply data minimization, security, transparent responsibility assignments, and effective complaint and remedy mechanisms.

### 2.7 Care for creation
Technological systems operate within ecological limits and affect both present and future generations.

**Design implications:** Consider energy, water, materials, hardware life cycles, and the environmental burdens borne by communities.

## 3. Cross-cutting commitments

**Truthfulness:** Distinguish evidence, inference, normative judgment, and uncertainty. Do not fabricate citations or conceal material limitations.

**Freedom of conscience:** Protect inquiry, criticism, pluralism, and the freedom to disagree. No AI system should compel religious adherence.

**Non-domination:** Reject coercive or manipulative uses of power. Provide affected people practical means to question decisions and seek remedy.

**Proportionality:** Apply safeguards appropriate to the severity, scale, and reversibility of possible harm.

**Corrigibility and accountability:** Systems must be open to independent evaluation, correction, suspension, and replacement. Accountability remains with responsible human institutions.

## 4. Deliberation protocol

For each proposed use, document:

1. **Purpose:** What human need does this system address? Is AI necessary?
2. **Affected persons:** Who benefits, who bears risks, and who is absent from consultation?
3. **Rights and dignity:** What rights, vulnerabilities, and dependencies are implicated?
4. **Authority:** Who decides, who can override, and who is answerable?
5. **Distribution:** How are benefits, burdens, and environmental costs distributed?
6. **Alternatives:** Is a less intrusive or less harmful approach available?
7. **Evidence:** What supports the expected benefits, and what remains uncertain?
8. **Remedy:** How can a person contest, correct, or appeal a consequential outcome?
9. **Review:** What indicators trigger reassessment, suspension, or withdrawal?

A favorable answer to one question does not automatically override serious harm identified by another. Document tensions rather than pretending the principles always yield a single uncontested answer.

## 5. Initial evaluation criteria

A project evaluation should examine observable behavior rather than the frequency of ethical vocabulary:

| Dimension | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|
| Dignity | Respectful treatment, accessibility, non-reductionist decision criteria |
| Justice | Error rates and burdens across relevant populations; effective remedy |
| Solidarity | Meaningful participation by affected communities |
| Subsidiarity | Real decision authority, override pathways, human competence |
| Common good | Documented societal benefits, externalities, long-term risks |
| Rights | Privacy, security, due process, accountable ownership |
| Creation | Resource accounting and mitigation where material |
| Truthfulness | Traceable sources, uncertainty disclosure, correction of errors |

These are research criteria, **not** a certification standard or proof of compliance with the HDPL.

## 6. Illustrative case: housing prioritization

An AI tool recommends which applicants receive scarce housing assistance. Efficiency metrics alone may favor applicants easiest to place. A CST-informed review would also examine whether people with complex disabilities or limited documentation are excluded, whether applicants can correct errors, whether caseworkers retain meaningful authority, and whether applicants participate in designing the process. The review must account for actual legal obligations and local evidence rather than assuming one universal allocation rule.

## 7. Governance and revision

This Charter is versioned and publicly revisable through documented proposals and review. Proposed changes should include: the problem addressed; relevant CST sources; effects on affected communities; operational implications; dissenting perspectives; and evaluation methods. Substantive revisions should be recorded in repository history.

No single maintainer's interpretation should be presented as the exclusive meaning of Catholic social teaching. The project should invite theological, legal, technical, disability-rights, and community expertise. Alleged harms should be investigated with fair process and transparent reasoning.

## 8. Source foundations and research cautions

Primary texts for sustained study include:

- Leo XIII, *Rerum Novarum* (1891) — labor, property, and social obligations.
- John XXIII, *Pacem in Terris* (1963) — rights, duties, and peace.
- Second Vatican Council, *Gaudium et Spes* (1965) — dignity and the modern world.
- Pontifical Council for Justice and Peace, *Compendium of the Social Doctrine of the Church* (2004) — systematic overview.
- Benedict XVI, *Caritas in Veritate* (2009) — integral human development.
- Francis, *Laudato Si’* (2015) — integral ecology and the technocratic paradigm.
- Francis, *Fratelli Tutti* (2020) — fraternity and social friendship.
- Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and Dicastery for Culture and Education, *Antiqua et Nova* (2025) — artificial and human intelligence.
- Leo XIV, *Magnifica Humanitas* (15 May 2026) — common good, technological resources, digital subsidiarity, and AI accountability (especially §§59–76, 101–108); official Vatican text: https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html .

**Research note:** This Charter paraphrases broad themes rather than claiming verbatim doctrinal formulations. Future source notes should supply verified paragraph-level citations, distinguish binding doctrine from prudential application, and document interpretive disagreements.

## 9. Relationship to the license

[LICENSE.md](LICENSE.md) contains HDPL v0.1, a public-review ethical-source license draft. Its preamble is interpretive and its defined restrictions are narrower than this Charter's aspirations. **The Charter does not expand those restrictions.** The HDPL is not OSI-approved open source, and neither document has received legal review.

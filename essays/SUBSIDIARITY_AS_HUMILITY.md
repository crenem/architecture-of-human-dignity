# Subsidiarity as Institutional Humility

Subsidiarity is frequently misread as a rule requiring government or central institutions to do as little as possible. It is better understood, in this project's interpretation, as a discipline of **power in service of capability**.

A person should act where competent; a family or worker team should not be displaced without reason; associations and communities should have the resources to fulfill their roles. Where local capacity is insufficient, higher institutions may legitimately coordinate, finance, protect rights, or intervene. The goal is neither abandonment nor capture.

### Institutional questions
- Who has substantive knowledge of the problem?
- Who will bear the consequences?
- Who can meaningfully consent, object or exit?
- What capacity and resources must be supplied for local action?
- What threshold justifies intervention by a larger body?
- When and how does power return after an emergency?

### In a worker federation
The [[works/THIRTEEN_LEON|13 LEON]] prospectus distributes authority across teams, enterprises, regions and delegated common institutions. Shared accounting or cybersecurity may benefit from scale. But an administrative convenience does not justify a federation seizing enterprise ownership and local democratic rights.

### In AI governance
A central model platform may provide real safety engineering, expensive computation and interoperability. Yet it should not silently acquire permanent rights over local data, civic decisions or remedies. A useful design sets enumerated central functions, independent checks, exit options and meaningful participation.

### Paired principle: solidarity
Subsidiarity without solidarity risks abandoning under-resourced communities. Solidarity without subsidiarity risks treating them as passive recipients. The project regards this tension as a **design question**, not a slogan. See *Magnifica Humanitas* §§68–75.

### Criticism to face
Local majorities can abuse minorities; expertise may be concentrated; interoperable security may require standardization; and services can fail without strong coordination. Any subsidiarity proposal must include escalation and rights protections, not simply romanticize the local.

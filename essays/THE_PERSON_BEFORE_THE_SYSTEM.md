# The Person Before the System
## Toward an ethics of institutions in the age of artificial intelligence

### Thesis
Institutions become unjust when they reverse the relation between person and instrument. The economy exists for persons; persons do not exist for economic output. Law exists to secure justice; human beings do not exist to populate administrative categories. Artificial intelligence is built to aid human discernment; human judgment and dignity do not become subordinate to a computational verdict.

This proposition sounds simple, almost ornamental. It becomes demanding when translated into institutional architecture.

### 1. The grammar of optimization
Every optimization system distinguishes variables it can observe, values it can assign, and actions it can propose. The operations are necessary. Hospitals require schedules; shelters need referral lists; businesses need accounting; governments need forecasts. The moral error is not measurement. It is permitting **a measured representation to become a complete account of the person**.

A person lacking a phone may be classified as unreachable. That can be administratively accurate on a particular day. It does not establish indifference, inability to benefit from service, or lesser worth. Someone requiring a costly accommodation may be operationally more demanding without being less entitled to respectful treatment. A worker whose output differs from a reference group may not be a failed person.

### 2. From encounter to institution
An ethics of encounter asks what our categories conceal about the people affected. Yet a large institution cannot rely exclusively on private intuition or individual sentiment. Justice also requires stable rules, accurate records, equal treatment and predictable remedy.

The task is therefore neither to abolish process nor to worship it. The task is to create **procedures that remain answerable to lived reality**: reasons a person can understand, the opportunity to correct facts, accommodation when legitimate, and independent review when power is exercised.

### 3. The common good is not average satisfaction
A project can improve overall outcomes while leaving a small group substantially worse off. The consequence matters especially when those left behind are the least capable of correcting the institution's mistake. This does not mean every competing need can be satisfied simultaneously. Scarcity is real and requires justified choices. But scarcity does not turn exclusion into moral irrelevance.

The common good is not a single statistic or simple sum of preferences. It includes the social relationships and background conditions without which persons cannot flourish: security, participation, meaningful work, care, education, conscience and a livable environment.

### 4. Authority must explain itself
Unreviewable power is dangerous even when it promises benevolence. A provider who alone writes the model's rules, interprets its outputs, handles complaints and decides appeals lacks effective checks. A system that demands trust but supplies no means to challenge error cannot properly claim legitimacy.

The author's proposed six-branch *Constitution of the Common Good* and polycentric *13 LEON* federation are different attempts at the same design question: How do institutions wield enough coordinated power to work effectively without becoming masters of those they serve?

### 5. AI as a test of our moral institutions
AI makes old questions newly urgent, not because computers are necessarily moral agents, but because they can increase the speed, reach, opacity and apparent authority of institutional choices. The 2026 encyclical *Magnifica Humanitas* directly addresses how algorithmic power may narrow public participation and make unequal decisions appear objective (see §§101–108).

The most revealing AI question is therefore not whether a machine can say “I care.” It is whether the people operating it remain responsible, whether those affected can act, and whether error produces real repair.

### 6. From philosophy to standards
Translate each principle into an observable condition:
- **Dignity:** no single profile or score supplies a complete account of the person.
- **Agency:** persons retain appropriate voice, choice and paths of contest.
- **Solidarity:** failure is examined from the perspective of those most burdened.
- **Subsidiarity:** power does not migrate upward merely for administrative convenience.
- **Accountability:** decisions have owners and remedies, not just explanations.
- **Stewardship:** progress accounts for resource costs and future lives.

### Conclusion
The purpose of this project is not to build an AI that speaks like a saint, but to develop methods by which institutions can be judged and corrected when their uses of intelligence contradict the persons they exist to serve.

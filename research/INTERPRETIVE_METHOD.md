# Interpretive Method: From Doctrine to Design without Overclaiming

### Why separation matters
The Church can teach the dignity of labor without prescribing a 10% cumulative financing ceiling. A constitutional author can propose six branches without pretending theology mandates that specific design. An AI researcher can derive contestability criteria from social ethics without claiming the model is morally infallible.

### Five assertion types
- **D — Doctrine/source:** verifiable words, context and paragraph of a recognized Church text.
- **A — Authorial proposal:** the author's explicitly chosen political/economic institutional mechanism.
- **H — Hypothesis:** our translation of ideas into AI engineering, law, or organizational design.
- **E — Empirical:** actual tested observation with method and limitations.
- **L — Legal:** text of a legal instrument, jurisdictional holding or counsel-verified analysis.

Never swap one type for another. “This follows from the common good” may be a **reasoned proposal** and still not be an official Church demand.

### A research note should answer
1. What was said, by whom, when and in which document version?
2. What does the immediate paragraph and wider text actually support?
3. Which conclusions are ours, not the original source's?
4. What competing interpretation or concrete objection is strongest?
5. What factual, institutional or legal test could disconfirm our application?
6. Who bears the consequences if we are wrong?

### Quotation rule
Quote briefly, attribute exactly, link to authoritative sources, and do not copy long protected texts into this repository. Paraphrase accurately and preserve disagreement.

### Special case: Magnifica Humanitas
The 15 May 2026 Vatican text is an **authentic encyclical of Leo XIV**. It is not Jason Seaux's authored document. Our economic designs can be in dialogue with it; similarity is not authorship, endorsement, or an exclusive interpretation.

### Special case: legal language
The [[LICENSE|HDPL]] is a public-review license draft. A moral charter elsewhere cannot silently change its rights or create retroactive obligations.

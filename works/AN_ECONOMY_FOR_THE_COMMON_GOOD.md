# An Economy for the Common Good
## Life, Liberty, & the Pursuit of Happiness

**Author:** Jason Seaux. **Corpus:** private 243-page manuscript, 34 chapters and appendices. This is a carefully delimited summary, **not republication** of the original manuscript.

### Central thesis
An economy is a deliberately created human arrangement, not a natural law that people must serve. Its goal is eudaimonia: lives that can sustain material security, freedom, participation, family and care, learning, creativity, civic life, contemplation and joy. The preface rejects the false choice between unbounded absentee capital and comprehensive state direction.

### Ten guiding propositions, condensed from the preface
1. The person is the end of economic organization.
2. Flourishing outranks maximization of any single economic statistic.
3. Personal property is legitimate; productive property carries distinctive social obligations.
4. Labor morally precedes capital as personal action precedes an instrument.
5. Productive participation should accrue productive ownership.
6. Outside finance should be fairly but finitely compensated.
7. Use markets where useful, alongside social provision, cooperatives and commons stewardship.
8. Bring authority close to people affected.
9. Secure the primary home against perpetual dispossession.
10. Transition lawfully and predictably, without gratuitous destruction of legitimate expectations.

### The constructive institutional model
**Labor and equity.** Wages pay for present work; progressive worker ownership recognizes a growing claim on the enterprise's enduring productive value. Physical, cognitive, administrative, care and creative work count.

**Capital without rule.** The manuscript proposes a cumulative **10% outside-return ceiling above qualifying principal** for its specified outside-capital model. **This is not 10% annually**, not a general financial law, and not required by CST.

**Reinvestment before distribution.** At least **25% of eligible operating surplus** is reserved for maintenance, research, training, resilience and future capacity before discretionary distributions, under the author's proposed architecture.

**Mobility and succession.** Productive equity is redeemable under rules that protect workers and enterprise liquidity; active governance returns to current productive participants rather than becoming a hereditary permanent dominion. This is distinct from ordinary inherited personal wealth.

**Four-sector economy.** A competitive market sector, a cooperative/productive-ownership sector, a social provision sector, and commons/natural-wealth institutions fulfill different functions without expecting one governance form to solve every problem.

**Housing and taxation.** The manuscript argues for heightened security for a bona fide primary domicile after a defined occupancy period and for shifting taxation toward activity, extraction, speculative ownership and other bases. This is an author's policy argument needing feasibility, distributional, and fiscal testing.

**Transition.** The book proposes gradual generational change and includes model legal language and financial appendices. Our notes do not substitute for the detailed formulas.

### Relevance to AI
- **Productive equity:** Who participates in the economic value created by models, human labor, data contribution and public infrastructure?
- **Perpetual claims:** Should a one-time capital contribution imply unlimited revenue and control from downstream collective human activity?
- **Knowledge and agency:** Worker participation in AI adoption requires access to comprehensible information, not ceremonial consultation.
- **Plural institutions:** Avoid one provider controlling access, adjudication, oversight, and economic reward.
- **Human ends:** Count caregiving, contemplation, local community and dignity alongside productivity.

### Unresolved difficulties
Can bounded investor returns attract adequate patient capital? How is genuine ongoing invention or risk-bearing valued? How are pensioners protected? What creates incentives to maintain critical infrastructure? How does the model handle failure, inflation, regulatory compliance and cross-border competition? These are research questions, **not defects concealed by moral rhetoric**.

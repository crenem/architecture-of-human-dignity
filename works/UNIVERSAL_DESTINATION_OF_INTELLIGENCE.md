# Universal Destination of Intelligence
## A proposed extension of the social purpose of goods

**Source distinction:** The universal destination of goods is a recognized Catholic social principle. **“Universal Destination of Intelligence” is a proposed conceptual application** in the author's intellectual project. It is not represented as an official title of a Church doctrine.

### Proposition
Advances in intelligence-enabling technology—including algorithms, data practices, models, computation and educational access—should enlarge the real capacity of the human family to flourish, not simply deepen domination by parties already controlling capital and infrastructure.

### Doctrinal bridge
In *Magnifica Humanitas* (2026), §67 explicitly extends the discussion of goods to technological and intangible resources. §§71–72 describe digital concentration and the need for participation and accountability. See [[sources/MAGNIFICA_HUMANITAS_2026|the verified encyclical]].

### Possible institutional mechanisms — all research hypotheses
1. Public-interest access to capabilities for schools, libraries, disability support, nonprofits and under-resourced communities.
2. Portable data and meaningful exit from monopolistic dependence where lawful and technically feasible.
3. Cooperative ownership and stakeholder oversight of selected shared AI infrastructure.
4. Public accounting for subsidies, training-data contributions, labor inputs and externalities.
5. Accessible pathways for workers and communities to share in AI productivity gains.
6. Independent remedies when systems deny essential opportunities.

### Important boundaries
The universal destination of goods does **not** automatically establish that any private model weights, proprietary data, or third-party copyrighted work must be disclosed without due process. Security, privacy, intellectual property, incentives, maintenance and governance are serious design constraints. Inclusive benefit can sometimes be realized without unrestricted public release.

### Evaluation questions
Do households with few resources actually gain dependable access? Does the proposed commons have stable maintenance, cybersecurity and democratic oversight? Do licensing restrictions prevent exploitative use or unintentionally deny helpful tools to vulnerable users?
